From the first proton on lithium to a world model of a neutron source

Published 27 November 2026 Abhinav Jha, Co-founder & Director

In 1932, in Cambridge, John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton fired protons from a machine they had built into a lithium target and split its nucleus. It was the first nuclear reaction made with artificially accelerated particles. Ninety-four years later, a proton striking lithium is again where our work begins.

The linear accelerator is a few years older. In 1928, at Aachen, Rolf Widerøe built the first working one and showed that a particle can be accelerated in steps along a straight line, without ever needing the full voltage at once. After the war, surplus radar hardware turned the idea into machines: Luis Alvarez's proton linac at Berkeley in 1947, and the first electron linacs at about the same time. In 1953 a linear accelerator began treating patients at Hammersmith Hospital in London, and the hospital linac is now found in thousands of cancer centres. At the end of the 1960s Kapchinsky and Teplyakov proposed the radio-frequency quadrupole, which made compact, high-current proton and ion linacs practical and is the first stage of most of them today.

India has been part of this story almost from the start. Around 1940 Meghnad Saha set out to build a cyclotron in Calcutta. The war intervened, and it gave its first beam only in 1953, the year those first patients were treated in London. The Variable Energy Cyclotron in Kolkata, designed and built in India, gave its first beam in 1977. The Pelletron accelerators in Mumbai and New Delhi were each extended with a superconducting linear booster developed largely in India. SAMEER's first hospital linac went into service in Chandigarh in 1991. Indore has the Indus synchrotrons. At Trombay the LEHIPA proton linac (an ion source, a radio-frequency quadrupole and drift-tube linacs) has taken protons through all its stages to full energy, as the front end of a far larger machine planned for accelerator-driven systems. And India builds for the world's machines too, among them a diagnostic beam for ITER.

What strikes me in this history is how little of the time went into the idea. Again and again the machine was understood on paper long before it did what the paper said. The years went into commissioning: showing by calculation and by measurement that the source, the beam, the shielding and the application behave as designed. It is still so. By the vendors' own announcements, the accelerator-based BNCT system in Helsinki needed about six years from installation to its first trial patients, and the one in Xiamen nearly four to its Phase I trial.

I started this work because I think that time can be shortened if the whole chain is held in one model. We are building a connected model that runs from the nuclear data, through the neutron source and the transport of neutrons and photons, to the application at the far end. In the language of physical AI it is a world model of a neutron source: fast learned emulators of each link, trained on simulations we can trace to their data, checked against measurement, and updated when the real facility differs from the design. A commissioning team could then ask "what if" in seconds and spend its measurement time where the model is least certain. We are starting with proton–lithium sources for boron neutron capture therapy.

The same chain serves the other sources we are designing. Alongside BNCT, these are compact, switchable neutron sources for Indian production lines: to image the hydrogen-rich ordnance and seals inside the metal pyrotechnic casings of space hardware, alongside X-ray inspection; to measure the elemental composition of the raw mix on cement and mineral conveyor belts, with a source that turns off when it is not in use; to test memory and logic chips for the upsets that neutrons cause; and to show where the lithium and the electrolyte sit inside a sealed battery cell without opening it. Each is a source, a shield and a measurement that has to be commissioned, and each can draw on the same model.

Fusion is where this is meant to go. In deuterium–tritium fusion the neutrons carry four-fifths of the energy, so much of the engineering is knowing where they go. The connected model is the shared foundation of every step on our path: after the accelerator source comes a linear magnetic mirror, whose plasma is itself a steady neutron source. The source changes; the chain behind it does not.

What we are releasing now is an early step, and a deliberately small one. Before we trust a learned emulator of our own physics, I wanted to know whether an automated research loop can be made to work under rules that anyone can check: a public benchmark, a comparison against tuned models, a test scored once, every attempt recorded. On NVIDIA's public Linear Radiation Transport dataset the loop improved a model under those rules, and the release says plainly where the rules fell short.

Shortening commissioning is the thesis we are testing. The early signs are encouraging. Our internal work is now taking the same method to our own calculations, and we will be releasing more of this research soon.
